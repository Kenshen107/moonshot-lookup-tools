Two things every trade-in and every purchase come down to: what condition is this card actually in, and is it actually real? Both guides below are meant to be thorough enough to make the call yourself, with the visual tells laid out plainly.

Why Grading Matters

Condition is the single biggest swing factor in what a card is worth after rarity and demand. A $40 Near Mint card can drop to $15-$20 if it's beat up - so grading it correctly, consistently, protects both the store and the customer on every trade-in and every sale. This guide uses TCGplayer's official Card Conditioning Standard - the same five-tier scale (Near Mint → Damaged) used industry-wide by TCGplayer, most singles vendors, and most local game stores - so a grade called here means the same thing anywhere else a customer takes the card.

Quick method: hold the card up at an angle under a bright light and look across the surface (not straight down at it) - whitening, scuffs, and scratches catch the light and jump out far more than they do under flat overhead lighting. Check front AND back; the back shows wear (especially corner and edge whitening) more readily than the front art does.

The Five Grades (TCGplayer Standard)

Definitions below are TCGplayer's own official language. Photos are real graded Magic cards from a TCGplayer-standard grading reference published by MTG Seattle - click any photo to open the full front/back/close-up filmstrip at full size and compare it side by side with whatever you're grading.

Near MintNM

Cards in Near Mint condition show minimal wear from shuffling, play, or handling and can have a nearly unmarked surface, crisp corners, and unblemished edges outside of a few slight flaws. A Near Mint card may have slight edge wear or a couple minor scratches, but overall looks nearly unplayed with no major defects.

Lightly PlayedLP

Cards in Lightly Played condition may have minor edge wear, scuffs, or scratches. There are no significant imperfections or issues with the structural integrity of the card. Noticeable imperfections are okay, but none should be too severe or at too high a volume.

Moderately PlayedMP

Cards in Moderately Played condition can have edge wear, surface wear, scratching or scuffing, bends with creases, or a combination of moderate examples of these imperfections. Bends/peels/liquid exposure may be acceptable here as long as they don't compromise the card's structural integrity.

Heavily PlayedHP

Cards in Heavily Played condition show a major amount of wear - a variety of moderate imperfections along with creasing, whitening, and other faults. HP cards can have severe imperfections, but must still be authenticatable and cannot be structurally damaged.

DamagedDMG

Cards showing wear beyond the other conditions: major border wear, scratching/scuffing, bends, tears, splitting, or foreign substances (food, liquid, etc.) that impact the card's integrity. Cards with any foreign substance can't be sold through TCGplayer Direct or Marketplace listings at all. No standard reference photo exists for this tier since damage varies too widely to picture one example - use the definition above and the "structural integrity" test in the callout below.

TCGplayer's structural-integrity test, in three questions: does the imperfection affect the card's Structure (is it physically stable), its Playability (can it be shuffled/sleeved/used as a game piece), or its Authentication (can the manufacturer's security markers still be verified)? A "yes" to any of those is Damaged, regardless of how minor everything else about the card looks.

What Counts As What (TCGplayer's Measured Definitions)

TCGplayer's full standard (see their Comprehensive Guide) grades each individual imperfection by exact size/length, not just a vibe - this is the level of detail that separates a confident grader from a guess. Selected highlights:

  • Bend/Crease: A bend up to 10mm with no crease can still be NM-adjacent (Lightly Played cap). Any actual crease - a line or ridge from folding - caps the card at Moderately Played, no matter how faint. A bend over 20mm caps it at Heavily Played; one that compromises structure is Damaged.
  • Edgewear (whitening/fraying on the border): Measured by contiguous length along the edge. Up to 20mm is allowed at Near Mint; 20-80mm caps at Lightly Played; 80-160mm caps at Moderately Played; over 160mm caps at Heavily Played.
  • Surface Wear (whitening that exposes the cardstock underneath, not just the color layer): Not allowed at all at Near Mint - even a small 2.5mm² patch caps a card at Lightly Played. Beyond 40mm² the card is Damaged.
  • Scratches: Short scratches (under 40mm long) are acceptable at any condition including Near Mint. Longer or denser scratch clusters ("scuffing") push the grade down further, and scuffing that covers an entire side of the card ("total scuffing") caps at Heavily Played.
  • Indentations/dings: A single pinpoint indent is fine at Near Mint. Anything larger, or any indent visible from the other side of the card, caps progressively lower down to Heavily Played.
  • Grime (dirt/smudges from handling): Small amounts are fine through Moderately Played. Anything beyond ~27.5mm² of surface grime is Damaged. A visible stain, sticky residue, or anything that could be a biohazard is a Foreign Substance instead - Damaged outright, and not eligible for sale as a normal listing at all.
  • Centering: A card can be off-center up to 70/30 without it affecting condition. Beyond that, it's Damaged regardless of how clean the card is otherwise.

These are the exact thresholds TCGplayer sellers are held to, straight from their published standard - handy for settling a "that's not really LP" disagreement at the counter with something more concrete than opinion.

What To Check, In Order

  1. Corners. The single most common wear point. Look for whitening (the black/colored border wearing down to show the white cardstock underneath) and for corners that are bent, soft, or rounded off instead of sharp.
  2. Edges. Run your eye down all four edges under angled light for whitening or fraying (tiny loose fibers along the border). Edge whitening on a card that traveled loose in a binder or box is extremely common.
  3. Surface. Tilt the card and look for scratches, scuffing (a dulled patch where the gloss has been rubbed away), and print lines. Genuine light manufacturing print lines run in one consistent direction from the printing press and are normal even on NM cards - they're not damage.
  4. Structure. Flex the card VERY gently (or just look at it edge-on under light) for creases. A crease that only shows as a faint line when flexed is minor; one that's visible flat and unflexed, or that you can feel as a ridge, pushes the card down at least to MP-HP.
  5. Centering. Check against the 70/30 threshold above. Also matters a lot for anyone planning to send the card to a third-party grader (PSA/BGS/CGC), where centering alone can cap the maximum numeric grade regardless of how clean the card is otherwise.
  6. Back of the card. Always flip it over. Backs show whitening and scuffing faster than fronts because the uniform blue makes any wear far more visible - a back check will often catch damage a front-only glance misses entirely.

Judgment Calls Worth Knowing

  • Foils grade tougher. The foiling process makes even NM foils prone to natural waviness/curling straight from the pack - light, even curling on a foil is normal and not automatically a grade-down, but any actual crease still is.
  • Old-border (pre-8th Edition) cards often have naturally softer corners from the print stock of the era even in NM copies - compare against other cards from the same set/era rather than a modern card's crisper corners.
  • When in doubt, grade down one tier, not up. A customer unhappy that their card graded LP instead of NM is a much smaller problem than a customer who bought an "NM" card that clearly wasn't.
  • Sleeve residue and dust are not damage - wipe with a dry microfiber cloth before grading. Never use liquid cleaners on a card; that can warp it or strip the finish.

Sources: TCGplayer's Card Conditioning Overview and Comprehensive Imperfections Guide; example photos via MTG Seattle's condition guide.

Counterfeits exist at every price point, not just Power 9 and dual lands - reprint-boosted staples, popular Commander cards, and even bulk rares get faked because they're printed and sold at volume. Check anything unfamiliar, regardless of price. No single test below is 100% conclusive on its own - run more than one, and always compare against a card you already know is genuine.

1. Weight

A genuine Magic card weighs 1.7-1.8 grams on a precision scale. Fakes almost always deviate, since counterfeiters use different cardstock/materials - modern fakes commonly run heavier, around 1.9g, since they tend to use more plastic than genuine cardstock.

Real - 1.76g
Fake - 1.85g

A cheap 0.01g precision scale is all this takes. A slight deviation either way is normal manufacturing variance - it's cards noticeably outside the 1.7-1.8g band that are worth a second look.

2. The Light Test

Compare the suspect card to a genuine card of the same set/color held up to a bright light - never judge a card against light alone, always side by side with a known-real one.

2a. The core layer, viewed from the edge

Look at the card's edge under a magnifier while backlit. A genuine card's middle layer shows a bluish tint (most sets have this "blue core" - a few older sets like Ice Age legitimately don't). Fakes commonly have a fully black core instead, and their edges look unusually crisp/"sharp" since fakes are die-cut in small batches rather than mass-produced.

Real - blue core
Fake - black core

2b. Light passing through the whole card

Hold the card flat between a light source and your eye. A genuine card lets through an even, moderate amount of light on both sides of the image. Fakes tend to run to an extreme in either direction - either far more opaque/dim than a real card, or letting noticeably more light through than a real card would.

Real
Fake

3. Print Quality Under Magnification

A cheap 20x jewelry loupe (or a phone macro lens) is enough for all of these. Genuine cards print colors as a fine dot-by-dot, layer-by-layer rosette pattern first, then add the black border/text/symbols as a separate sharper "top layer" pass on top of it - a level of print precision counterfeits consistently fail to replicate.

3a. Rosette (dot) pattern

Real
Fake

Real: dots and lines sharp as a knife, text framing clearly distinct from the rosette. Fake: dots blurry and chaotic, no clean distinguishable pattern.

3b. Set/mana symbol "top layer" separation

Real
Fake

Real: the black lines of the set/mana symbol sit cleanly on top of the color dots. Fake: the symbol's black lines are made of dots directly intermixed with the color layer underneath - no separation between them.

3c. Straight lines (e.g. the border around "Deckmaster" on the back)

Real
Fake

Real: lines stand out sharp and clean against the background. Fake: lines look pixelated, uneven, and slightly blend into the background instead of standing apart from it.

4. Card-Back Security Details

Three specific, well-known tells hiding in plain sight on every card's back - all quick to check with just a loupe, no light needed.

4a. The Green Dot

Inside the green dot on the card back, there's a bright yellow spot. Inside THAT spot, genuine cards have four tiny red dots arranged in an L-shape, and the green dot itself is ringed by a solid, saturated black circle.

Real
Fake

4b. The "T" in "The"

In "Magic: The Gathering" on the back, look closely at the "T" in "The." On a genuine card, the left edge of the T is a smooth straight line while the bottom and right edges are subtly wavy. On a fake, all the edges look the same - no distinction between straight and wavy.

Real
Fake

4c. The "Demon Horns" in Deckmaster

Above each of the two "E"s in "Deckmaster" on the card back, genuine cards have tiny crescent-shaped marks (nicknamed "demon horns"). They're small and easy to miss, but they're consistently missing on fakes - even ones that otherwise look convincing.

Fake

Foil pattern and modern holofoil stamp

Real foils have a fine, even foiling pattern across the whole card face; fakes often show a foil layer that's patchy, only partially applied, or scratches off to reveal plain cardstock underneath. Since War of the Spark (2019), genuine cards also carry a small black-and-gold holofoil Wizards stamp near the bottom edge of modern-frame cards - its absence, or a stamp that looks flat/printed rather than foiled, is a red flag (pre-2019 frame cards legitimately have no stamp).

Alpha/Beta "rebacks" and cut-downs

An old trick on early sets: gluing an International/Collectors' Edition card face onto a genuine card's back to fake a Beta card, or rounding Beta corners to mimic Alpha's. Rebacks fail the weight test badly (glue adds mass) and show adhesive residue or "warping" like water damage under a loupe; cut-down corners look irregular compared to a genuine card's clean die-cut corner lip.

Double-sided cards (newer trend)

Printing a convincing back removes an easy tell, so lean harder on the other tests here - especially print quality/rosette pattern and the core-layer check, since these are trickier to fake on both sides at once. A glossier, more "plasticky" feel than a comparable genuine card is also common on these.

If You're Still Not Sure

  • Compare it directly against Scryfall's own high-resolution scan of that exact printing (use the card's own printing/set, not just any copy - misprints and different printings can legitimately look slightly different from each other).
  • Collector Boosters and Wizards Promo Packs are printed at different facilities worldwide (Japan/Europe/US) and can legitimately vary in core-layer color and weight from each other - a "too heavy" or "purple instead of blue" core alone isn't damning if the other tests (Green Dot, Demon Horns, rosette pattern) all check out.
  • For anything high-value (Power 9, dual lands, Reserved List cards), when in doubt, don't buy/accept it on the spot - most counterfeit rings target exactly these high-dollar cards because a wrong call is expensive.
  • Third-party grading companies (PSA, BGS, CGC) authenticate as part of their grading process - a slabbed card from a reputable grader is a strong (though not 100% infallible) signal of authenticity.

None of these tests are individually 100% conclusive on their own - a suspect card failing any one of them is reason enough to hold off, but a card passing one test isn't a guarantee either. When something feels off, ask a staff member before completing a trade or purchase.
